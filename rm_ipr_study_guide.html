1. Meaning of Research — Definition

★★
Definition (memorise one clean version)

Research is a careful, well-defined, objective, and systematic search for knowledge — driven by curiosity about something unknown and useful — so as to make an original contribution that expands the existing knowledge base.

Add for 4–5 mark questions:

  • It involves formulating a hypothesis, analysing data, making deductions, and checking whether conclusions fit the hypothesis.
  • It is the process of creating knowledge that does not yet exist.
  • Thus research is "an art of scientific investigation."

★★

Interest, challenge, learning, meaning, purpose. Linked to strong creative performance.

Rewards — money, fame, awards, praise, status. Strong motivators but may block creativity. (e.g. a patent makes you rich and famous.)

03

Solving unsolved problems, intellectual joy, service to community, respectability.

Wanting to do better than the world has, improving state of the art, contributing to society, fulfilling historical legacy.

Government directives, funding opportunities, terms of employment.

5. Ethics in Engineering Research + 3 Ways to Credit Contributions

★★
Definition

Ethics = a set of rules distinguishing acceptable from unacceptable conduct — right from wrong.

  • Learnt early but moral development continues; everyone shares norms but interpretation differs.
  • Ethics ≠ laws, but laws often follow ethics because ethics are shared values.
  • International norm milestone: Nuremberg Code, 1947.
  • Per Whitbeck, research-credit issues trace to the British Royal Society (17th century), which gave priority to whoever first submitted findings.
  • Research ethics = examines the appropriate application of research outcomes.
  • Responsible conduct of research = the way the work is undertaken. (These are NOT interchangeable — common exam trap.)
3 Ways Credit is Attributed in Publications
1. Authorship — of the intended publication
2. Citation — of previously published/presented work
3. Acknowledgement — of inputs to the present research

6. Types of Research Misconduct

★★★ (Asked repeatedly)
F1

Fabrication

Illegitimate creation of data. Making up data/experiments because you "know" the result but can't wait (timeline pressure).

F2

Falsification

Inappropriate alteration of data. Misrepresenting/altering real data to fit a desired hypothesis.

P

Plagiarism

Taking others' work without attribution. Self-plagiarism (reusing your own published work) is also misconduct. A low similarity score ≠ no plagiarism — human eye needed.

OA

Other Aspects

Fraud (deception + damage); simultaneous/double submission; not reporting known mistakes.

MOD 02

Literature Review, Citations & Knowledge Flow Knowledge-flow diagram appears almost every paper

1. Importance of Literature Review & Technical Reading

★★★ (5+5 marks split)

Why Literature Review Matters

  • Use existing content/ideas/approaches to correctly identify the (vaguely known) problem and advocate a suitable approach.
  • Confirms your research will contribute something new and innovative.
  • Quality judged by: appropriate breadth & depth, clarity, rigor, consistency, effective analysis.
  • Two-step process: (i) identify major topics/subtopics/concepts; (ii) place each source's citation in the correct category.
  • Continuous and cyclical — you go back and forth till the project ends.

Technical Reading — Skimming Order

  • 1. Title & keywords → if not interesting, stop.
  • 2. Abstract → overview in minimum time; if not important, stop.
  • 3. Conclusions → check relevance.
  • 4. Figures, tables, captions → quick broad idea.
  • 5. Introduction → background + why they did the study.
  • 6. Results & Discussion → the heart of the paper.
  • 7. Experimental setup/modelling → only if deeply interested.

2. New & Existing Knowledge — Foundation Relationship

★★
  • New knowledge can only be interpreted within the context of what is already known — it cannot exist without the foundation of existing knowledge.
  • Existing knowledge is needed to prove a problem exists and is important (context, significance, originality, tools).
  • You show your knowledge is original by describing what already exists and pointing out the missing part.
  • A strong foundation (lots of references) makes your new knowledge harder to challenge.
  • Textbooks usually hold older established knowledge; research papers hold newer work. Read textbooks first.

3. Bibliographic Databases + Web of Science

★★
Definition

Bibliographic databases = "abstracting and indexing services" that collect citation info and abstracts and make them searchable. Searching several large databases avoids over-relying on one.

Web of Science Features

  • Formerly ISI / Thomson Reuters; includes multiple databases + specialized tools. Needs institutional license.
  • Search by title, topic, author, address (dropdown fields).
  • Sort by number of citations (high→low) or publication date.
  • Refine by material type, date, language.
  • "Cited reference search" — trace articles that cited an earlier paper.

Google vs Google Scholar

  • Google: great to start; finds free govt/org reports. Limits: "black box," no quality control, limited refinement.
  • Google Scholar: limits to scholarly literature. Limits: some results aren't truly scholarly; not comprehensive.
  • Search operators: OR (broadens), ( ) brackets, " " quotes (exact phrase), site:, filetype:pdf/ppt/xls

4. Citations — Definition + Functions + 4 Failing Types

★★★
The Trio — Memorise
Citing = quoting from / referring to others' works in the text so the context is clear.
Referencing = listing the full publication details of a cited work.
Acknowledgment = indicating contributions to the work.

Cite each source twice: (i) in-text where quoted/paraphrased; (ii) in the references list at the end.

3 Main Functions of Citation

  • Verification function — lets readers check if the original source justifies the claim.
  • Acknowledgment function — researchers get credit; affects promotion, employment, funding, reputation.
  • Documentation function — documents scientific concepts and historical progress of a technology.

4 Citations That FAIL Their Purpose

  • Spurious — adding a citation when none is required or appropriate.
  • Biased — citing friends with no connection, or refusing to cite genuinely significant work.
  • Self-citations — citing your own prior work; acceptable only if genuinely relevant.
  • Coercive — editors pressuring authors to add citations to the editor's own journal (boosts impact factor).

6. Impact of Title & Keywords on Citations

★★
  • Title is the most important attribute — main indicator of the subject, used during literature survey, helps marketing and traceability.
  • A good title is informative + attention-grabbing (some are one but not the other).
  • Three aspects shape title behaviour: type of title, length of title, presence of specific markers.
  • Longer titles (with methodology/results) → more attention and citations.
  • Titles with a question mark, colon, or specific geographical region → lower citation rates.
  • Result-describing titles get more citations than method-describing ones.
  • Review and original articles get more citations than short communications.
  • At least two keywords in the title increases discovery and citations.
  • Citation styles: ASCE (American Society of Civil Engineers) and IEEE (Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers).

1. Intellectual Property — Definition + Types

★★★ MUST KNOW
IP Definition

Intellectual Property is the terminology for intangible assets having commercial value, arising from human intelligence, creativity, and imagination, but typically lacking physical form.

IPR Definition

The privileges accorded to the creator/inventor in accordance with law, given in exchange for revealing the creation in the public domain — granting rights to use, sell, distribute, offer for sale, and restrict others from using it without permission.

1. Copyrights & Related Rights

  • Books, music, architecture, carvings
  • Films, software, databases

2. Conditions / Criteria for a Patent — Section 2(1)(j), Patents Act 1970

★★★ (Asked very often)
01

Novelty

"Not part of the State of the Art." The innovation is new and not known anywhere in the world:

  • Not in public knowledge
  • Not published anywhere by any means
  • Not claimed in any other specification by any other applicant
02

Inventive Step

"Not obvious to a person skilled in the art." The innovation:

  • Is a technical advancement over existing knowledge
  • Has economic significance
  • Is not obvious to a person skilled in that subject
03

Capable of Industrial Application

"For the benefit of society." The invention can be made or used in any industry.

5. Rights of Patentee + Enforcement + Infringements

★★

Rights of Patentee

  • Owner decides who may or may not use the invention.
  • Invention cannot be made, used, distributed, imported, or sold by others without consent.
  • Owner may permit others on mutually agreed terms.
  • Patent rights are "negative rights" — owner restricts others rather than gaining a positive right to make. (Important exam phrase.)

Enforcement & Infringements

  • Owner may sue infringers and seek compensation.
  • Patent rights enforced by judicial courts; court can stop infringement.
  • Main responsibility for monitoring lies with the patent owner.
  • Direct infringement — product substantially close to patented product, or marketed/used commercially without permission.
  • Indirect infringement — some deceit or accidental infringement without intent.
  • Reliefs: interlocutory/interim injunction; damages or account of profits; permanent injunction.

Copyrights Copyright registration flowchart is highest priority

1. Definition of Copyright

★★★ MUST KNOW
Definition

Copyright is the legal right given by law to the original creator of work in the fields of literature and computer software. The "Related Rights" cover work in dramatics, sound recording, film/video recordings, paintings, architecture, etc. Both are governed by the Copyright Act, 1957 of India.

Four Rights Provided by the Act
1. Reproduction  ·  2. Communication to the public  ·  3. Adaptation  ·  4. Translation

2. Classes of Copyright

★★★ MUST KNOW — easy listing marks
01

Literature

Books, essays, research articles, speeches, lectures, computer programs, software, databases

02

Dramatics

Screenplays, dramas

03

Sound Recordings

Recording of sounds on any medium (phonogram, CD-ROM)

04

Artistic

Drawing, painting, logo, map, chart, photographs, architecture, engravings

05

Musical

Musical notations (excluding words/actions sung with music); need not be written down

06

Cinematograph Film

Visual recording by any medium including a sound recording (motion pictures, TV programs)

3. Criteria for Copyright

★★★ Heavily Favoured
01

Physical/Tangible Form

It exists in some physical/tangible form — even scribbled notes count.

02

Originality (OWA)

Created from the author's own independent thinking, void of duplication — Original Work of Authorship (OWA). May resemble existing works but must not be the same.

03

Creative Effort

At least some creative effort — no minimum threshold; subjective, judged by the Registrar of Copyright. Quality/quantity/aesthetic merit don't matter.

Key Counter-Examples

Merely changing a book's dimensions = NOT copyrightable. An alphabetical phone directory = NOT copyrightable (no creative selection).

4. Ownership of Copyright — Four Rules

★★
  • The person who created the work is the first/original owner.
  • If the author is an employee contracted by a proprietor, the proprietor owns it.
  • For government work, the government is primary owner (absent any arrangement).
  • The person delivering a speech is the first owner.

5. Copyrights of the Author — Section 14

★★★ Important

Economic Rights (Proprietary)

Owner can authorize/prohibit:

  • Reproduction
  • Distribution of copies
  • Public performance
  • Broadcasting/communication
  • Translation
  • Adaptation (e.g., novel → screenplay)

Moral Rights (Personal)

  • Right of Paternity — author can always claim authorship even after copyright is transferred (name stays).
  • Right of Integrity — author can prevent misuse/distortion that harms his honour or reputation.
Note

A single work can have multiple rights holders (e.g., a song has lyricist, composer, singer, musicians, recorder).

6. Copyright Infringement — Criminal & Cognizable

★★★ Favoured

10. Definition of Trademark

★★★ MUST KNOW
Definition

A trademark is a unique symbol capable of identifying and differentiating the products/services of one organization from others. "Mark" = a sign, design, phrase, slogan, symbol, name, numeral, device, or combination. Essentially anything that identifies a brand to a consumer.

11. Eligibility Criteria for Trademark

★★★ Favoured
01

Distinctiveness

Must be unique enough to identify the source of goods/services in the market.

02

Not Merely Descriptive

Must NOT merely describe the goods/services. Descriptive marks usually aren't protected, unless they acquire secondary meaning (e.g., "Apple" for electronics).

03

No Similarity to Prior Marks

Must not be similar to existing registered marks.

12. Designation of Trademark Symbols

★★★ Easy marks

16. Famous Case Law: Coca-Cola vs. Bisleri

★★★ Easy self-contained marks
Case Study — Trademark Infringement

MAAZA — The Mango Drink Dispute

  1. MAAZA (mango drink) was a registered trademark of Indian company Bisleri International Pvt. Ltd.
  2. Bisleri transferred the rights (formulation, IPR, goodwill) to Coca-Cola for the Indian territory.
  3. In 2008, Bisleri registered 'Maaza' in Turkey and began exporting under that mark.
  4. Coca-Cola filed for permanent injunction and damages for passing-off and infringement.
  5. Coca-Cola argued the mark was assigned to them, so manufacturing under it — whether for India sale or export — was infringement.
  6. Verdict: Court granted an interim injunction against Bisleri from using MAAZA in India and for export — held to be infringement.

17. Definition of Industrial Design

★★★ MUST KNOW
Definition

A 'Design' = the features of shape, configuration, pattern, ornament, or composition of lines or colours applied to any article. Can be 1D, 2D, 3D, or a combination. Created by any industrial process (manual/mechanical/chemical) and judged solely by the eye in the finished article. It does NOT include any mode/principle of construction or a merely mechanical device.

Famous Examples
Coca-Cola contoured bottle (1915)  ·  iPhone body
  • Governed by: The Designs Act, 2000 and Design Rules, 2001 (amended 2008, 2013, 2014, 2019).
  • A design should be: novel & original, applicable to a functional article, visible on the finished article, with no prior publication.

19. Design Rights & Enforcement

★★★ Favoured

Design Rights

  • Registration gives a monopolistic right — owner can legally exclude others from reproducing, manufacturing, selling, or dealing in the registered design without consent.
  • Useful where product shape has aesthetic value.

Enforcement

  • Owner can sue for piracy of a registered design.
  • Case filed in a court not lower than District Court.
  • Court first confirms the design is registered under the Act — if not registered, no legal action.
  • If guilty, infringer pays damages of ₹50,000 per registered design infringed.

20. Non-Protectable Designs (Excluded Matter)

★★★ Favoured
  • Designs against public moral values
  • Flags, emblems, signs of any country
  • Integrated circuits
  • Designs describing the "process of making an article"
  • Books, calendars, certificates, forms, dressmaking patterns, greeting cards, leaflets, maps, postcards, stamps, medals
  • Artistic works under Section 2(c) of Copyright Act (paintings, sculptures, drawings, photographs, architecture)
  • Trademarks
Duration

Registered designs are protected for 10 years, extendable by 5 years (total 15) via renewal application (Form-3) with fee before the initial 10 years expire.

25. Apple vs. Samsung Case

★★ Know Briefly
Case Study — Design & Utility Patent Infringement

iPhone 3GS vs Galaxy i9000

  1. 2011: Apple sued Samsung in the US (N. District of California) for infringing designs and utility patents (screen app grid, tap-to-zoom).

30. Turmeric (Curcuma) Patent Case

★★★ Easy marks — self-contained
Case Study — Biopiracy / Traditional Knowledge

USPTO Patent Revocation — 1997

  1. In 1995, the USPTO granted a patent to the University of Mississippi Medical Center for the use of turmeric in wound healing.
  2. India's CSIR (Council of Scientific & Industrial Research) challenged it, arguing this was traditional knowledge used in India for centuries — not novel.
  3. CSIR presented documentary evidence: ancient Sanskrit texts and an old published paper.
  4. The USPTO revoked the patent in 1997 — a landmark win for protecting traditional knowledge.

Neem Patent Case

★★
Case Study — Biopiracy

EPO Patent Revocation — 2000/2005

  1. The European Patent Office granted a patent (to USDA and W.R. Grace) on a fungicide derived from neem oil.
  2. India challenged it as traditional knowledge (neem long used in India as a pesticide/medicine).
  3. Patent was revoked in 2000/2005.

Basmati Patent Case

★★
Case Study — Geographic Origin / Traditional Knowledge

RiceTec USPTO Partial Revocation — 1997–2001

  1. US company RiceTec got a patent on "Basmati rice lines and grains" in 1997.
  2. India challenged it; the USPTO struck down most claims.
  3. This protected the Basmati name as linked to the Indian subcontinent.
Collective Significance
These three cases collectively show why India built the Traditional Knowledge Digital Library (TKDL) — a database of India's traditional knowledge to prevent future biopiracy.
